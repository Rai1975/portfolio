# The Human Experience
Humans and computers have been interacting for a while now. We co-exist. What the human is bottlenecked by, the computer can do. This is essentially the entire moat of anything in computing.

Automation is everywhere, we automate everything.

The goal is to bring us closer to what we actually want. _Democratize the human experience_.

For example, google maps democratized knowledge of geography and travel. Leading to the death of the travel guide, but the democratization of curated independent travel and exploration [1]. I don't need to know how the transit system in Taiwan works, but I sure as hell know google maps will get me to where I want to go! 

We're vigorously cutting down every single bottleneck we find in getting closer to the human experience. We are biohacking to live longer, optimizing software to find us results quicker and expanding network trying to get every square inch of this planet connected to the internet.

And here we are today, with _language based reasoning intelligence in our pockets_. Now, we're terrified. "AI Will take my job!!". But what if that was the point? Hear me out.

# The Virgin Pen

To see why, go back about five hundred years.

"The pen is a virgin," wrote Filippo de Strata in the late fifteenth century, but "the printing press is a whore." [2] 

Infuriated by the _sudden automation of his craft_, di Strata wrote letters to the Doge of Venice demanding a total ban on the printing press. He claimed that printers were uneducated "asses" corrupting literature, called the printing press a "whore" that cheapened knowledge, and argued that mass-produced books would make the public lazy and illiterate.

Does that sound familiar? This is the same _friction_ we are seeing today. 

And it is completely valid, we are presented with technology that can completely flip how the world works today upside-down. 

Being alive today, we know that the printing press did not make the public lazy and illiterate, it empowered even more literature. _It moved the bottleneck from who could copy words to who had something worth saying._ I’m watching the same shift happen in my own work.

As a CS kid, this is insane. AI can write and test code at speeds I can't even fathom. Programming is a craft, it gave me the canvas to bring my ideas into tangible products. But now, _the code isn't the bottleneck to bringing my ideas to life - it's me_. And that's a beautiful thing! I may not need excellent coding skills in the future because there stands fewer things in between my idea and a product. 

However, there’s an uncomfortable side to being the bottleneck. If the machine handles the part I used to be good at, all that’s left is the part that was always mine: knowing what to build and why. What if work was what gave my life meaning, and I just handed it over?

# What's Mine?
Maybe the answer is that the meaning was never in the typing. It was in the problem I wanted solved, and the code was the toll I paid to get there. Di Strata mourned the scribe’s pen, but the pen was never the point. The ideas were. The press just made it impossible to hide behind the copying.

So the bottleneck is moving, and it’s landing on us. I don’t know what the next one looks like, and neither does anyone else. But I think the question worth sitting with is this: _if the tedious part of your work disappeared tomorrow, what would you spend the time on? Whatever you answered is probably the part that was always yours._ 

That’s what democratizing the human experience was supposed to mean: not removing the work, but clearing the way to the part that’s yours.
